The plan

Seven questions to ask on every problem. One running example walks through all of them: picking 3 of 5 friends for a sleepover.

  1. What am I making? Write one.
  2. What is being chosen, and from what?
  3. Different kinds, or a rule? Split into cases, or start with the most restricting part.
  4. Swap two chosen things. Is it a new outcome?
  5. Build it one decision at a time. Same number of options each time? Multiply.
  6. Did I build any outcome more than once? Divide by the copies.
  7. Check a tiny case you can list.

A group without order

If swapping two picks gives the same thing, you're counting groups, and each group hides once in every order.

From class. Example 1.1 (01:50–06:57, captures 3–6): the teacher lists the six pairs from A, B, C, D by 'groups with A, then with B but not A…', then counts 4 × 3 = 12 ordered and boxes AB = BA, AC = CA… to show the 12 come in twos. He renames 'combinations with order' to 'groups with order'.

More worked numbers. Pairs from 9: 9 × 8 ÷ 2 = 36. Pairs from 20: 20 × 19 ÷ 2 = 190.

The choose function

Choose k from n = (k picks in a row) ÷ (the k! orders of one group).

Groups in a row

Fill the groups one after another, choosing each from whoever is left.

From class. Example 1.4 (22:39–29:20, captures 15–18): 'The choose function is not usually a single end to a problem… it might represent one step.' He fills the 4-bag, then the 3-bag from the 5 left, then the last 2, and explains 2 choose 2 = 1 using 0! = 1. He calls 9!/(4!3!2!) the 'multichoose function' (the usual name is multinomial coefficient).

More worked numbers. 7 kids into clubs of 4 and 3: 7 choose 4 = 35. 5 kids into clubs of 2, 2 (named), 1: 10 × 3 × 1 = 30.

Identical same-size groups

If groups are the same size and have no names, swapping them changes nothing, so divide by the ways to name them.

From class. Example 1.5 (29:20–36:48, captures 19–23): he writes 'Answer: 252?' and crosses it out — 'it's not a mistake, it's simply an incomplete solution' — then draws A–E | F–J and F–J | A–E in one yellow oval: 'these arrangements are the same'. Identical bags are 'sort of like identical A's'.

More worked numbers. 8 kids into two unnamed teams of 4: 70 ÷ 2 = 35. 9 kids into three unnamed teams of 3: 1,680 ÷ 6 = 280.

Block walking

Every up/right path is a word of U's and R's; choose where the U's go. Through a checkpoint, multiply the two legs.

Handle the rule first (choose the allowed groups, then the members), or split into kinds that can't overlap and add.

Tricky bits

Each trap comes from a real problem in the set, with the tempting wrong answer.

  • Order sneaks in (Problem 1)
    720 Counting 10 × 9 × 8 feels finished, but it lists the same handful in every order.
  • Dividing by k instead of k! (Problem 1)
    240 Three candies feel like 3 copies. Write the orders of A, B, C: there are 6.
  • The forced last team (Problem 2)
    15,876 Once the team of 5 is picked, the team of 4 has no choices left: × 1, not × 126.
  • Different sizes never twin (Problem 2)
    63 Halving out of habit. A team of 5 can't trade places with a team of 4.
  • Unnamed look-alike teams (Problem 3)
    252 It's the right count for teams called Red and Blue. These teams have no names.
  • Painted letters (Problem 4)
    5,040 7! treats the three U's as three different letters.
  • Adding the legs (Problem 7)
    161 Two numbers, so add them? A trip needs leg 1 AND leg 2, so every pairing counts.
  • Forgetting the checkpoint (Problem 7)
    12,870 Every A-to-C path, including all the ones that miss B.
  • Three teams, divide by 3 (Problem 8)
    11,550 Three teams feels like ÷3, but three teams can be named in 3! = 6 ways.
  • Adding the copies (Problem 9)
    207,900 Dividing by 3! + 2! = 8. Each pair-naming goes with each trio-naming, so the copies multiply: 12.
  • The spare job (Problem 11)
    480 One job each, then a spare. But which of the doubled-up kid's jobs was the 'spare'? Each assignment got built twice.
  • Sides aren't diagonals (Problem 13)
    28 Every pair of corners, including the 8 neighbours that make sides.
  • 0 can't lead (Problem 14)
    240 Treating increasing and decreasing the same. An increasing number can't contain 0; a decreasing one can end in 0.
  • Any three blocks (Problem 15)
    2,300 25 choose 3 ignores the row and column rule.
  • Faces don't double (Problem 16)
    256 Each coin up or down looks like 2 choices each, but a face-up coin can't sit under a face-down one.

Cheat sheet

SituationMove
Grab or pick a group of different thingsSwap test: same group? Then n choose k.
Several groups filled one after anotherChoose each from whoever is left; multiply.
Same-size groups with no namesCount with names, then divide by m! (m look-alike groups).
Groups of different sizesThey can't trade places: no extra division.
Repeated lettersChoose the spots for one letter (or divide by each letter's copies).
Up/right paths on a gridCount the R's and U's; choose the U spots.
Path must pass a checkpointSplit at the checkpoint; multiply the legs.
Shapes made from points or linesChoose what decides the shape: corners, endpoints, or a pair of lines from each family.
A rule like 'at most one per state'Start with the most restricting part: choose the allowed groups first.
'At least one of each' or 'or'Split into kinds that can't overlap; add them.
Taking most of a setCount the few left behind instead (take = leave).
The order inside the answer is forced (like increasing digits)Just choose the set; the order comes free.
An unknown nMake a table of n, or cancel the factorials and look for consecutive numbers.
Your count feels too bigWrite one outcome and look for its twins. Never divide just because the number feels large.

Glossary

Combination
A group without order: {A, B} is the same combination as {B, A}.
Ordered list
A selection where position matters: AB and BA are different lists. The rule of product builds ordered lists.
Set
A collection where only membership matters. Written with curly brackets: {5, 2} = {2, 5}.
Ordered pair
Two things in a fixed order, written with round brackets: (5, 2) is not (2, 5).
n choose k
The number of groups of k you can choose from n different things. Written as a tall bracket, C(n, k), or nCk.
Factorial
n! = n × (n − 1) × … × 2 × 1, the number of ways to line up n different things.
0! = 1 (the empty product)
There is exactly one way to line up nothing: do nothing. That's why 2 choose 2 = 2!/(2!·0!) = 1.
Multichoose
The teacher's name for 9!/(4!·3!·2!), splitting into groups of given sizes. Most books call it a multinomial coefficient; some books use 'multichoose' for something else (choosing with repeats).
Distinct / identical
Distinct things can be told apart (cookies A to J). Identical things can't (two plain bags, three U's).
Not necessarily distinct
Repeats are allowed but not required: 'three not necessarily distinct digits' includes 557.
Twins (our word)
Two builds that make the same real outcome, like AB and BA for one pair.
Round robin
A tournament where every pair of players plays once.
Diagonal
A segment joining two corners of a polygon that aren't neighbours.
Case
One kind of outcome that can't overlap with the other kinds; cases are added.

Write-up habits

  1. Write the choose form first, like C(10, 3), then the factorial form, then cancel. The teacher does this every time.
  2. Cancel the larger factorial in the denominator first: 10!/7! becomes 10 × 9 × 8.
  3. Pull small factors out of big ones before multiplying: 'four can come out of eight, leaving two'.
  4. Name each decision in words above its number: 'first bag', 'second bag', 'A to B', 'B to C'.
  5. A product is a finished answer: 2⁵ × 20 × 49 × 47 × 46 is fine. The checker accepts it.
  6. In equation problems, say what the letters stand for and what they must be: 'b and r are positive whole numbers'.
  7. Pause and try first. Every class problem began with 'pause the video'; the workbench opens the steps before any worked answer.
  8. Finish with a tiny case you can list. If the list and the rule disagree, the rule is wrong.
