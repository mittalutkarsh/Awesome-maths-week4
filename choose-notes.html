The plan

One way of thinking for every problem this week, in four steps. One running example walks through all of them: picking 3 of 5 friends for a sleepover.

  1. Tag every object with a letter that says where it goes.
  2. Count the words the tags spell: n! ÷ (each letter's repeats)!. Two separate words multiply; separate cases add.
  3. Look-alike groups (same size, no names)? Write the group word and divide by its repeats!.
  4. Check a tiny case you can list.

Words with repeated letters

A word's letters can be shuffled n! ways, but shuffling identical letters changes nothing, so divide by each letter's repeats!.

From class. Practice 4 (43:24–45:30, capture 30): the teacher writes 7!/(3!4!) for U, U, U, R, R, R, R and notices it 'looks like seven choose three'. That one observation is the whole method: every problem this week becomes a word like this.

More worked numbers. AAABB: 5! ÷ (3! × 2!) = 10. XXYYYY: 6! ÷ (2! × 4!) = 15. MISSISSIPPI-style words work the same way.

Choosing: tag T or L

Choosing k things from n is a tag word with k T's (take) and n − k L's (leave).

From class. Examples 1.1–1.3 and Proposition 1.1 (01:50–19:35, captures 3–13): the teacher counts in order, then removes the order (÷ 2 for pairs, ÷ 3! for {A, D, G}). With 12 marbles he notes that taking 10 'is the same as leaving 2'. The tag word builds the order removal in from the start.

More worked numbers. Teams of 3 from 6: 6! ÷ (3! × 3!) = 20. Leave 2 of 20: 20! ÷ (18! × 2!) = 190.

Tags for several groups

When everything is split into named groups, tag each object with its group's letter and count the words.

From class. Example 1.4 (22:39–29:20, captures 15–18): the teacher fills the 4-bag, then the 3-bag, then the 2-bag and arrives at 9!/(4!·3!·2!), which he calls the 'multichoose function'. That is exactly the tag word XXXXYYYZZ.

More worked numbers. 7 kids into clubs of 4 and 3: 7! ÷ (4! × 3!) = 35. 5 kids into named pairs and a single: 5! ÷ (2! × 2!) = 30.

Look-alike groups

Groups with the same size and no names can trade places, so write the groups as a word and divide by its repeats!.

From class. Example 1.5 (29:20–36:48, captures 19–23): the teacher writes 'Answer: 252?', crosses it out ('not a mistake, just an incomplete solution') and says identical bags are 'sort of like identical A's'. The group word is that idea written down.

More worked numbers. 9 kids into three unnamed teams of 3: 1,680 tag words ÷ 3! = 280. 8 kids into two unnamed teams of 4: 70 ÷ 2! = 35.

Paths are words

Every up/right path is a word of R's and U's, so count the words. Through a checkpoint, the two legs are two words: multiply.

From class. Example 2.1 and the 8 × 8 practice (45:30–50:22): the teacher writes a path as UURRUUURRRR, 'total number of movements, choose some', and splits A → C at B into 'two separate pieces'.

More worked numbers. 5-by-3 grid: 8! ÷ (5! × 3!) = 56. 3-by-3 grid through (1, 1): 2 × 6 = 12.

Tag what decides the shape

Find what decides the shape (corners, endpoints, or lines) and tag those T or L.

From class. Example 1.6 and Problem 3.1 (36:48–40:09, 50:22–54:06): 'I'm not going to choose the triangle itself. What I'm going to choose is whatever makes a triangle.' For parallelograms he numbers the slanted lines 1–6 and the flat lines 1–5.

More worked numbers. Triangles from a hexagon's corners: 6! ÷ (3! × 3!) = 20. Shapes in a 2 × 2 grid: 3 × 3 = 9.

Two words, or two cases

If a choice has two separate parts, it's two words: multiply. If it splits into kinds that can't overlap, count each kind and add.

From class. Problems 3.2 and 3.3 (54:06–66:09): 'Let's start with the most restricting aspect… choose the states first', and 'when we have separate cases like that, we think about those cases separately and then just add them together at the end.'

More worked numbers. 5 states of 2, choose 2 finalists: 10 state words × 4 = 40. Mixed teams of 3 from 4 red and 1 blue: only 2R + 1B, 6 × 1 = 6.

Tricky bits

Each trap comes from a real problem in the set, with the tempting wrong answer.

  • Order sneaks in (Problem 1)
    720 Counting 10 × 9 × 8 feels finished, but it lists the same handful in every order.
  • Dividing by k instead of k! (Problem 1)
    240 Three candies feel like 3 copies. Write the orders of A, B, C: there are 6.
  • The forced last team (Problem 2)
    15,876 Once the team of 5 is picked, the team of 4 has no choices left: × 1, not × 126.
  • Different sizes never twin (Problem 2)
    63 Halving out of habit. A team of 5 can't trade places with a team of 4.
  • Unnamed look-alike teams (Problem 3)
    252 It's the right count for teams called Red and Blue. These teams have no names.
  • Painted letters (Problem 4)
    5,040 7! treats the three U's as three different letters.
  • Adding the legs (Problem 7)
    161 Two numbers, so add them? A trip needs leg 1 AND leg 2, so every pairing counts.
  • Forgetting the checkpoint (Problem 7)
    12,870 Every A-to-C path, including all the ones that miss B.
  • Three teams, divide by 3 (Problem 8)
    11,550 Three teams feels like ÷3, but three teams can be named in 3! = 6 ways.
  • Adding the copies (Problem 9)
    207,900 Dividing by 3! + 2! = 8. Each pair-naming goes with each trio-naming, so the copies multiply: 12.
  • The spare job (Problem 11)
    480 One job each, then a spare. But which of the doubled-up kid's jobs was the 'spare'? Each assignment got built twice.
  • Sides aren't diagonals (Problem 13)
    28 Every pair of corners, including the 8 neighbours that make sides.
  • 0 can't lead (Problem 14)
    240 Treating increasing and decreasing the same. An increasing number can't contain 0; a decreasing one can end in 0.
  • Any three blocks (Problem 15)
    2,300 25 choose 3 ignores the row and column rule.
  • Faces don't double (Problem 16)
    256 Each coin up or down looks like 2 choices each, but a face-up coin can't sit under a face-down one.

Glossary

Tag
A letter written under each object saying where it goes: T or L, a team letter, R or U, a colour.
Tag word
All the tags read in order, like L T L L T L L T L L. Each outcome is exactly one tag word.
Look-alike groups
Groups with the same size and no names. They can trade places without changing anything.
Group word
The groups themselves written as letters: a letter per size, like A A A B B for three pairs and two trios.
Combination
A group without order: {A, B} is the same combination as {B, A}.
Ordered list
A selection where position matters: AB and BA are different lists. The rule of product builds ordered lists.
Set
A collection where only membership matters. Written with curly brackets: {5, 2} = {2, 5}.
Ordered pair
Two things in a fixed order, written with round brackets: (5, 2) is not (2, 5).
n choose k
The number of groups of k you can choose from n different things. Written as a tall bracket, C(n, k), or nCk.
Factorial
n! = n × (n − 1) × … × 2 × 1, the number of ways to line up n different things.
0! = 1 (the empty product)
There is exactly one way to line up nothing: do nothing. That's why 2 choose 2 = 2!/(2!·0!) = 1.
Multichoose
The teacher's name for 9!/(4!·3!·2!), splitting into groups of given sizes. Most books call it a multinomial coefficient; some books use 'multichoose' for something else (choosing with repeats).
Distinct / identical
Distinct things can be told apart (cookies A to J). Identical things can't (two plain bags, three U's).
Not necessarily distinct
Repeats are allowed but not required: 'three not necessarily distinct digits' includes 557.
Twins (our word)
Two builds that make the same real outcome, like AB and BA for one pair.
Round robin
A tournament where every pair of players plays once.
Diagonal
A segment joining two corners of a polygon that aren't neighbours.
Case
One kind of outcome that can't overlap with the other kinds; cases are added.
nk → n!k!(n−k)! → cancel

Write-up habits

  1. Write the choose form first, like C(10, 3), then the factorial form, then cancel. The teacher does this every time.
  2. Cancel the larger factorial in the denominator first: 10!/7! becomes 10 × 9 × 8.
  3. Pull small factors out of big ones before multiplying: 'four can come out of eight, leaving two'.
  4. Name each decision in words above its number: 'first bag', 'second bag', 'A to B', 'B to C'.
  5. A product is a finished answer: 2⁵ × 20 × 49 × 47 × 46 is fine. The checker accepts it.
  6. In equation problems, say what the letters stand for and what they must be: 'b and r are positive whole numbers'.
  7. Pause and try first. Every class problem began with 'pause the video'; the workbench opens the steps before any worked answer.
  8. Finish with a tiny case you can list. If the list and the rule disagree, the rule is wrong.
